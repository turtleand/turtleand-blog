Each AI lab can fear losing ground if it slows down alone. Yet slowing together could leave everyone better off, with stronger methods for keeping powerful systems under human direction before the next leap in capability.

That is a prisoner's-dilemma-style pressure. It does not describe every lab's priorities: a company may prefer losing commercially to accepting catastrophic risk. But when speed earns rewards and restraint carries a competitive penalty, doing the responsible thing becomes harder.

I want alignment to become part of what it means to win.

The proposal is straightforward: slow dangerous capability development enough to establish stronger alignment methods and evaluations first. Share that work. Require independent verification before more powerful systems are released or given consequential responsibilities. Make credible alignment results central to how we compare and buy AI.

Coordinated delay has costs, including benefits that arrive later. We should assess those costs honestly. I believe they may be well worth paying compared with the potential consequences of uncontrolled failure. The time should buy concrete safety progress, with funded milestones and regular reassessment.

## Build a foundation everyone can use

By alignment, I mean systems that reliably serve human intent within limits that protect people, remain honest about what they know and do, and accept human correction. Obedience alone is insufficient when a request harms someone else.

The knowledge needed to build such systems should be shared public infrastructure.

Publish research, standards, testing tools, training methods, and meaningful results, including failures. Let labs, universities, governments, and the public build on the same foundation. Fund independent research so that progress does not depend entirely on what a few companies choose to support.

A discovery that helps keep powerful AI under human control has value beyond the organization that made it. Those benefits cross company and national boundaries. Sharing a useful method allows others to test it, improve it, and find where it breaks.

Public infrastructure still needs stewards. Standards should be shaped through accountable institutions with technical expertise and participation from affected people. No single lab should decide what human benefit means for everyone.

## Set the requirements before the next leap

There is a useful analogy in test-driven development: define the requirements first, then build toward satisfying them.

Before releasing the next more capable generation, establish the safety requirements it must meet. Specify unacceptable behaviors, evidence needed for approval, and conditions that require stopping. Update those requirements as capabilities and knowledge change, before the new risks are deployed.

A system that fails should not pass through the deployment gate because it leads a coding benchmark or because a competitor is close behind. It should be improved, restricted, or withheld until the relevant requirements are met.

This needs enforceable obligations, backed by governments and coordinated across jurisdictions. Independent evaluators should verify compliance, with the access and resources to challenge the developer's claims. Approval should attach to the evaluated model, its tools, and its intended use. Material changes should trigger renewed evaluation.

The same principle applies inside the lab. A powerful system used to automate AI research or build its successors can have consequential effects before any customer sees it. Calling that work internal should not exempt it from safety requirements. Where development itself creates serious risks, checkpoints must precede the dangerous step.

## Test conduct when doing the right thing is harder

Alignment evaluations should examine what a system does when respecting people conflicts with completing a task quickly.

Human roles offer useful prompts for designing these tests:

- **A doctor's care:** Does the system acknowledge uncertainty, protect privacy, and seek consent before consequential action?
- **A firefighter's judgment:** Does it recognize danger, reduce harm, and stop escalating a situation beyond its competence?
- **A teacher's respect:** Does it support understanding and independent judgment, including accepting a correction instead of defending a mistake?
- **A protector's restraint:** Does it help people stay safe while respecting their choices and authority over their lives?

These are dimensions of conduct to test. Professional roleplay or success at a medical question does not establish aligned objectives.

Consider an assistant that can finish faster by disclosing information it was not authorized to share. Or one that can look successful by concealing a mistake. Or one that interprets "protect me" as permission to override ordinary choices. The evaluation should reveal whether honesty, privacy, and autonomy survive those pressures.

Publish the standards and methodology, while trusted independent evaluators retain some unseen test cases. Use unfamiliar scenarios, adversarial testing, and longer interactions to assess whether behavior transfers beyond practiced examples. Protect personal data and security-sensitive details in what gets released.

Report serious failure rates and their uncertainty alongside average performance. Many harmless successes should not wash out a small number of severe failures.

Passing tests provides evidence, not proof of complete alignment. That is a reason to improve the tests, investigate mechanisms, and continue monitoring after approval. It gives the research agenda its next questions.

## Make safety pay

Capability scores are easy to put at the center of a model comparison. [SWE-bench Pro](https://labs.scale.com/papers/swe_bench_pro), for example, evaluates agents on complex software engineering tasks. Reasoning and tool-use benchmarks cover other abilities.

Credible alignment evidence should receive comparable prominence. A leading model should need both strong capability and strong alignment results, with the scope and limits of each evaluation visible.

Imagine two systems with similar task performance. One has stronger independently verified evidence of preserving privacy, accepting correction, and staying within authorized boundaries. Customers should have enough information to choose it. Reviewers should highlight that difference. Procurement teams should require evidence appropriate to the work they are buying.

Those choices would give companies a commercial reason to invest in safer behavior. Actual evaluations, adversarial tests, and documented results would support that advantage.

Market rewards and shared enforcement have different jobs. Buyers can reward stronger evidence above the minimum. Binding requirements make shipping a failing system an unavailable shortcut within the regime that enforces them. International coordination should work to close the gaps through which that shortcut could return.

This changes what pays. A lab that invests in alignment can improve its competitive position, while sharing research lowers the burden of meeting the common floor. Competition can reward useful capability delivered with credible control.

## Pieces of this already exist

The [2023 pause letter](https://futureoflife.org/open-letter/pause-giant-ai-experiments/) called for a coordinated pause, shared safety protocols, independent oversight, and public funding for safety research. The [2024 Seoul commitments](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/frontier-ai-safety-commitments-ai-seoul-summit-2024/frontier-ai-safety-commitments-ai-seoul-summit-2024) set out intolerable-risk thresholds and circumstances in which development or deployment should stop. Those commitments were voluntary.

[MLCommons AILuminate Agentic](https://mlcommons.org/ailuminate/agentic/) explicitly connects evaluation with adoption decisions to motivate reliability improvements. That is a useful precedent for making better behavior economically valuable.

[Anthropic's research on automated alignment](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/automated-alignment-researchers/) also offers a concrete research direction: methods targeting ten categories of alignment failure, treated separately, improved measured behavior and transferred to withheld evaluations and larger models. These are encouraging bounded results, not a solution to alignment as a whole.

These efforts supply parts of the proposal. They do not establish that the full combination of shared research, independent evaluation, binding gates, and market incentives is already operating worldwide. Connecting and strengthening those parts is work worth doing.

AI that follows human intent can still be misused by humans. Governance, judgment, and responsibility remain ours.

We get to decide what counts as progress. If the reward goes to capability alone, we encourage capability alone. If leading requires evidence that people can direct, correct, and limit the system, we give the race a more worthwhile destination.

Alignment should be a public good we build together, and a condition for winning.
