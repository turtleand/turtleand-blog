A new model arrives. Someone publishes a workflow that seems to compress a week into an afternoon. Before you have tried it, another announcement appears.

You sit down to learn something and leave with the feeling that you are already behind.

There are real changes worth understanding. There are also demonstrations, predictions, and other people's priorities competing for the same attention. When all of them feel urgent, we lose the space needed to decide.

I think the useful response is to build a few anchors. Enough stability to keep learning without letting every new possibility choose our direction.

## 1. Keep a Direction That Survives a Release

What are you trying to become better at? Explaining difficult ideas? Building reliable systems? Serving a particular group of people?

Name something more durable than a tool. Then ask how a new capability helps that direction.

If the answer is unclear, you can leave it alone for now. An opportunity can be valuable without being yours to pursue this week.

**Anchor:** Write one sentence describing the capability you want to strengthen this month.

## 2. Turn Urgency Into a Small Test

A remarkable demonstration tells you what happened in that demonstration. You still need to discover what happens in your work, with your constraints and your definition of a good result.

Choose one bounded task. Try the tool. Examine the output, the corrections, and the time spent checking it. Decide whether to keep it based on that experience.

This gives curiosity somewhere useful to go. You leave with evidence instead of another unresolved possibility.

**Anchor:** Before adopting a tool, name the task and what would count as an improvement.

## 3. Protect the Ability to Judge

Delegation can expand what we produce. It can also make it tempting to approve things we cannot explain.

Keep contact with the reasoning that matters. Ask what assumptions hold the answer together. Check the parts whose failure would have consequences. Practice enough of the underlying work to notice when something feels wrong.

You do not need to perform every step yourself. You do need a way to take responsibility for the result.

**Anchor:** For one important output, explain why you trust it without quoting the model's confidence.

## 4. Give Attention a Boundary

Following every launch is an open-ended assignment. There is always another tab.

Choose when you will look for new developments and what deserves an interruption. Let the rest wait. Keep a short list of things to revisit rather than opening a new project for each interesting idea.

Missing an announcement for a few days is often a smaller cost than repeatedly abandoning work you already chose.

**Anchor:** Give discovery a place in the week and give focused work somewhere it cannot follow.

## 5. Measure What Remains

After a busy week, what is actually different?

A clearer explanation, a useful artifact, a corrected assumption, or a process you can repeat gives progress a shape. Hours of watching other people move can leave very little behind.

Include the ability to continue in that assessment. A pace that regularly consumes tomorrow's attention is expensive, even when today's output looks impressive.

**Anchor:** Review what you learned, made, and preserved, including your energy.

## A Small Experiment

For the next week, choose one meaningful outcome and one AI experiment that might help it. Keep interesting distractions on a separate list. At the end, review what changed before choosing the next experiment.

You can participate in this shift without treating every day as an emergency.

What would you choose to learn next if you were not trying to catch up with everyone?
